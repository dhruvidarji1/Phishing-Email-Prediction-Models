01 · Project Overview

Why phishing detection, and why now

Phishing is one of the most common cybersecurity threats, impacting individuals, businesses, and organizations. Email remains a primary channel of everyday communication, and phishing attacks are getting harder to recognize — messages are designed to look trustworthy and legitimate. AI can help by identifying suspicious emails faster and at a larger scale than manual review.

Research question

Which machine learning model provides the best balance of accuracy, precision, and recall for phishing email detection?

What makes this project stand out

  • Predicts phishing emails with 99.28% accuracy at its best.
  • Compares three distinct ML approaches head-to-head on the same feature set.
  • Reduces human error in spotting suspicious emails.
  • Designed to integrate into real-time email security systems.

How the proposal evolved

Original plan: use every available dataset column — this made fairly comparing the three models difficult, since some columns were repetitive or incompatible with certain model types.

Our solution: condense the dataset to a focused set of features — email text, length, punctuation, typos, sender domain, URLs, and attachments.

Result: a fairer comparison across all three models, making it easier to see each one's real strengths and weaknesses.

108,685 real-world emails, seven engineered signals

TaskBinary classification (0 = Legitimate, 1 = Phishing)
Key fieldsSender, receiver, subject, and email body

Feature engineering

Rather than feed in every raw column, we engineered a focused set of signals that generalize across all three model types:

Data visualizations

Class balance

Legitimate vs. phishing emails in the training set
  • The dataset is imbalanced: roughly 60K legitimate vs. 48K phishing emails.
  • Both classes are still large enough for reliable training and testing.

Phishing emails rely on links, not attachments

Share of emails containing each feature, by label
  • 81.8% of phishing emails contain a URL vs. 40.8% of legitimate — roughly double.
  • Attachments are rare in both classes, but slightly more common in legitimate mail (2.6%) than phishing (0.1%).
  • Takeaway: URL presence is a strong phishing indicator; attachments are not.

Legitimate mail is almost all plain text — phishing isn't

Email format composition, by label
  • Legitimate emails are 92% plain text, rarely HTML (3%) or mixed (5%).
  • Phishing emails split more evenly: 34% plain text, 38% HTML+plain mix, 28% HTML only.
  • HTML enables fake buttons, hidden links, and copied brand logos — not possible in plain text.

04 · Impact & Bias

How can building this model amplify or mitigate bias?

A phishing detector doesn't just need to be accurate — it needs to be fair across writers, industries, and cultures. We audited our training data and modeling choices for exactly that.

Sources of bias in our dataset

01
Temporal
Older emails don't reflect modern phishing tactics.
02
Class labeling
Possibly incorrect labels affect model accuracy.
03
Organizational
Mostly corporate settings, limited writing styles.
04
Language
Mostly English emails; less accurate elsewhere.
05
Cultural
North American norms, limited global representation.

Mitigation strategies

1. Balance and diversify the dataset
Collect emails from multiple sources, organizations, and providers so no single type of phishing or legitimate email is over-represented.
2. Evaluate and monitor for bias on an ongoing basis
Test the model on datasets with different writing styles, industries, languages, and formats; regularly track false positive/negative rates and retrain on the groups where performance is weakest.
Essential question

How can the process of creating AI/ML solutions amplify or mitigate bias in the case our group is using?

Comparing our three models shows that model choice directly affects bias: Logistic Regression and BERT (99.28% and 98% accuracy) better capture context and rely less on surface-level trigger words, while Naive Bayes (96.03%) is more prone to misclassifying senders who don't fit typical patterns. Because our legitimate-email data comes largely from Enron's 2001 corporate emails, even our strongest models risk false positives against non-native English speakers or informal writers, and false negatives against modern, AI-generated phishing. Choosing a higher-performing, context-aware model is one way the modeling process can reduce — though not eliminate — bias embedded in an imperfect training dataset.

05 · Next Steps

Where we're taking this next

  • Improve model accuracy and reduce false positives/negatives further
  • Train on more, and more diverse, data
